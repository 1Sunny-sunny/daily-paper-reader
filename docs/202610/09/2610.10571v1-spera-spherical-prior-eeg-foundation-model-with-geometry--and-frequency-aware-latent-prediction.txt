Geometry- and Frequency-Aware Latent Prediction
Minsu Kim1, Ye-Sung Kim2, Hyeseong Jeon2,
Wooseok Hyung2, Joshua Lee2, Chang-Hwan Im3∗

Abstract
Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a non-invasive measure of ongoing neural
activity, but building general-purpose EEG models remains challenging due to
the heterogeneity of subjects, devices, and electrode montages. Existing EEG
foundation models predominantly rely on reconstruction-based objectives defined
on the observed signal, which contains both neural and non-neural components.
We introduce SPERA† (Spherical Prior EEG Representation Architecture), an
EEG foundation model that adopts the joint-embedding predictive architecture
(JEPA) to predict in latent space. SPERA introduces a Legendre-polynomial spatial
prior, incorporated into attention to encode varying scalp electrode geometries.
Two further components adapt the model to EEG: factorized temporal and spatial
attention interleaved with periodic full-attention blocks, and a relational spectral
regularizer aligning latent similarity structure with spectral views. Pretrained on
approximately 80,000 hours of EEG from 29,048 subjects across 106 datasets,
SPERA achieves the highest average balanced accuracy across nine downstream
tasks spanning clinical, cognitive, and BCI applications. SPERA further exhibits
strong parameter efficiency under linear probing and robustness across varying
recording conditions, suggesting its potential as a general-purpose backbone for
diverse EEG analyses.

Introduction
Electroencephalography (EEG) reflects dynamic neural activity and underlying brain states. With
high temporal resolution and ease of acquisition, EEG is widely used across clinical diagnosis,
cognitive neuroscience, and brain-computer interface (BCI) applications [133, 88, 17, 146]. Deep
learning models have achieved strong performance in interpreting these signals [2, 120].
Despite this progress, building a general-purpose EEG model remains challenging due to the hetero-
geneity of EEG data. EEG signals vary significantly across individuals [121] and are recorded under
diverse experimental conditions with different devices and electrode configurations [19]. This makes
it difficult to learn representations that transfer across datasets and subjects.
Inspired by foundation models in vision and language [112, 40], recent EEG foundation models
such as LaBraM [57], CBraMod [150], and REVE [94] have explored scalable self-supervised
pretraining, using masked reconstruction or token-prediction objectives whose targets are derived
from the input signal. EEG recordings contain both neural activity and non-neural components such
as muscle and ocular artifacts. Reconstruction-based objectives define their prediction targets on all

the representation level. We therefore adopt the joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) [8].
We propose the Spherical Prior EEG Representation Architecture (SPERA), a representation learning
framework for EEG built on latent-space prediction. Beyond this objective, our central contribution
is a Legendre polynomial spherical prior incorporated into attention, providing a learnable pairwise
geometric bias that reflects the spherical structure of scalp EEG and applies to arbitrary montages.
Two further components adapt the backbone to EEG: factorized temporal and spatial attention

Building on these components, we pretrain SPERA on a multi-domain EEG corpus comprising
approximately 80,000 hours of recordings from 29,048 subjects across 106 datasets. We evaluate
SPERA on nine diverse downstream tasks spanning clinical, cognitive, and BCI applications and
compare it with prior EEG foundation models. We further investigate the scaling behavior of latent-
space prediction under EEG-aware design, with a focus on parameter efficiency under linear probing
(LP).
2
Related Work
Self-Supervised Objectives for EEG
Most existing EEG foundation models [57, 150, 94, 79, 75]
rely on masked modeling objectives with targets derived from the input signal, including reconstructed
signal patches and discrete neural tokens. Such targets therefore include both neural and non-neural
components of the observed signal. JEPA models [8, 9] instead predict in latent space, and several
EEG models have moved in this direction: latent prediction against a momentum-updated target
encoder [87], representation alignment paired with auxiliary patch reconstruction [149], generative
diffusion whose denoising activations serve as representations [110], and direct applications of JEPA
to EEG [45, 53, 95]. However, none of these explicitly encode the pairwise geometric relationships
between electrodes, which vary across montage configurations. We use a latent prediction objective
over a montage-variable token grid, with masking of temporal regions and spatial neighborhoods
defined by 3D electrode coordinates, and scale it to a multi-domain corpus spanning over a hundred
datasets.
Electrode Geometry in EEG Modeling
A central challenge for EEG foundation models is the
heterogeneity of electrode montages across datasets. BIOT adds channel embeddings and relative posi-
tion embeddings [155], CBraMod uses asymmetric conditional positional encoding to adapt to diverse
EEG formats [150], and REVE introduces a 4D positional encoding scheme for arbitrary electrode
arrangements [94]. Recent JEPA-style EEG models also introduce spatial inductive biases, including
3D coordinate-based positional embeddings [45], video-like spatio-temporal tokenization [53], and
coordinate-aware channel mixing with Fourier features [95]. We instead encode electrode geometry
directly into attention using a Legendre-based spherical prior, providing a learnable pairwise bias
derived from the biophysical geometry of the scalp [102, 21].
Spatio-Temporal Modeling in EEG
EEG signals exhibit distinct spatial and temporal dependen-
cies, motivating attention factorization along each axis. A related factorized design has been used in
video understanding, where TimeSformer and ViViT apply temporal and spatial attention sequentially
within each block [18, 7]. In EEG, CBraMod models spatial and temporal dependencies in parallel
using criss-cross attention [150], while CSBrain [162] combines cross-scale spatio-temporal tokeniza-
tion with structured sparse attention over local temporal windows and anatomical regions. State-space
models use Mamba-based backbones for EEG sequence modeling [151]. LuMamba uses learned-
query cross-attention to unify variable electrode configurations before temporal modeling [20]. These
approaches capture spatial and temporal structure in different ways. However, our setting introduces
two additional design considerations: EEG channel montages vary across datasets, unlike the fixed
spatial grids used in these video models, and purely factorized attention restricts direct cross-axis
interaction. We address these considerations by combining explicit electrode-geometry encoding
with a hybrid backbone that interleaves factorized and full-attention blocks.

Spectral Objectives in EEG Pretraining
Frequency components are key features of EEG signals,
motivating spectral objectives in several pretraining methods. LaBraM trains its tokenizer with vector-
quantized neural spectrum prediction [57], and Pradeepkumar et al. [109] introduce time-frequency
motif learning for frequency-aware EEG tokenization. We complement this line of work with a
relational spectral regularizer that aligns pairwise similarity structure between latents and spectral
views, without predicting absolute spectral values.
3
Methods
We denote an EEG recording as X ∈RC×T , where C is the number of electrodes and T is the
number of time samples. Each electrode is associated with a 3D anatomical coordinate, collectively
represented as E ∈RC×3. To tokenize X, each of the C channels is divided into Pt non-overlapping
1-second temporal patches at a sampling rate of fs = 200 Hz, resulting in a total of N = C · Pt
tokens. Each token is uniquely indexed by a (channel, time-patch) tuple (c, p) on the spatio-temporal
grid. Unlike token sequences in language or vision, EEG signals have dataset-specific spatio-temporal
grids, as channel montages vary across datasets. This cross-dataset variability and the two-axis
structure of the token space motivate the architectural choices described in this section.
3.1
Self-Supervised Objective: Spatio-Temporal JEPA
JEPA Objective
Figure 1 illustrates the overall pretraining framework. Raw EEG contains muscle
and ocular artifacts that persist after preprocessing, so an input-space reconstruction objective requires
reproducing them alongside neural activity. We adopt JEPA [8], which predicts latent representations
of masked target regions from visible context. SPERA consists of a student encoder fθ and a teacher
encoder fξ; the teacher receives no gradients and is updated as an exponential moving average (EMA)
of fθ. On the spatio-temporal grid, we partition the token indices into disjoint context and target
sets, C and T . The student encodes only the context tokens to produce Zctx. The teacher encodes
the full token sequence, and Ztgt is obtained by selecting the teacher outputs at the target positions.
A two-layer cross-attention predictor gϕ predicts ˆZtgt from Zctx. For each target position, a query is
formed by adding the target coordinate embedding to a single learnable vector shared across all target
positions, and is refined by cross-attending to Zctx. Following prior work [9], the model is trained to
minimize the ℓ1 distance in latent space:

Masking Strategy
For each sample, temporal masking is activated with probability 0.8, and spatial
masking is activated independently with probability 0.8. If neither is activated, we default to temporal
masking. Temporal masks follow a semantic subsequence preserving (SSP) strategy [87]: rather
than masking random individual time patches, we preserve contiguous blocks of patches to ensure
the context carries coherent temporal structure. Spatial masks select a random electrode as a center
and mask its k-nearest neighbors based on 3D Euclidean distance, with k = max(1, ⌊ρs · C⌉) for a
sampled spatial ratio ρs. Masking ratios are drawn uniformly from [0.15, 0.35] for time and [0.10,
0.30] for space (ρs).
Student Augmentation
We apply EEG-specific perturbations to the student’s context input: tem-
poral amplitude scaling and additive Gaussian noise, together with Gaussian jitter to electrode
coordinates. Augmentation hyperparameters are reported in Appendix C.1.
3.2
Encoder Architecture
Dense full attention allows interactions among all spatio-temporal token pairs but does not explicitly
distinguish temporal and spatial dependencies. We adopt a hybrid backbone of factorized attention
blocks interleaved with periodic full-attention blocks for cross-axis interactions.
Hybrid Attention Blocks
The base block factorizes attention along each axis separately. A
temporal attention pass operates independently over the Pt time patches within each channel, followed
by a spatial attention pass over the C channels at each time patch. Every fourth block is a full self-
attention block that attends jointly over all N = C · Pt tokens, enabling cross-axis interactions. Both
attention block types share standard transformer design choices: a pre-norm architecture with layer
normalization (LN), a SwiGLU MLP [128], and rotary position embeddings (RoPE) [139] applied
along the temporal axis (in both the encoder and the predictor).
3.3
Electrode Geometry Encoding with Spherical Prior
To handle varying electrode montages across datasets, we incorporate simple coordinate embeddings
and Legendre-based spherical priors. The Legendre prior is implemented in two forms: a pairwise
Legendre bias for factorized attention blocks and Legendre anchor encoding for full-attention blocks.
The two are assigned to attention types based on computational considerations analyzed in Section 4.5.
Coordinate Embedding
A direct approach to spatial conditioning is a per-token additive embed-
ding derived from electrode coordinates. We normalize each coordinate onto the unit sphere and pass
it through a linear projection. The resulting channel-wise embedding is scaled by a learnable scalar
α, initialized to zero, and shared across all temporal patches of the same electrode.
Legendre Spatial Bias
Scalp EEG electrodes lie approximately on a spherical head surface, and
we exploit this structure to build a biophysically motivated spatial prior on the sphere itself. Legendre
polynomials provide a natural basis for parameterizing pairwise functions of angular separation on
the sphere and have been used in conventional EEG analysis [102, 21]. We therefore use a truncated
Legendre expansion to encode pairwise electrode geometry as an additive attention bias.
Within each factorized attention block, the spatial attention pass computes attention over C channels
at a fixed time patch. To encode pairwise geometric relationships between electrodes, we introduce a
learnable additive attention bias bij between channel i and channel j, defined as a truncated Legendre
polynomial expansion of their cosine similarity on the unit sphere:

3.4
Relational Spectral Regularizer
Frequency components are key features of EEG signals, but incorporating spectral objectives into a
JEPA framework is not straightforward. Direct spectrum reconstruction would introduce an absolute
signal-derived target, whereas our goal is to regularize spectral relationships without reconstructing
spectral values. Therefore, we introduce a relational spectral regularizer (RSReg) that aligns the
pairwise similarity structure of the predicted representations with that of the corresponding spectral
targets. This design is inspired by relation-preserving distillation [98, 97], which supervises pairwise
relational structure. Here, the target relations are computed deterministically from spectral views of
EEG patches, not from another network.
For each target token (c, p), sc,p is the log-power spectrum of its 1-second raw patch, computed
via a Hann-windowed FFT using frequency bins within the 1–45 Hz band, and z-score normalized
across frequency bins. The predictor output ˆzc,p is passed through a small projector hψ to obtain
rc,p = hψ(ˆzc,p). We align the pairwise similarity matrices of r and s through a KL divergence loss:

Experiments
4.1
Pretraining
Dataset Collection
We collected a large-scale pretraining corpus spanning approximately 80,000
hours of EEG recordings from 29,048 subjects, yielding 2.67 million channel-hours and 9.60 billion
tokens in total. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest multi-domain EEG pretraining
corpus reported for a general-purpose EEG foundation model. The corpus is organized into three
categories (clinical, cognitive, and BCI), comprising 82.08%, 16.21%, and 1.71% of the total
recording time, respectively. The pretraining dataset exhibits substantial heterogeneity in electrode
montages, covering a wide range of channel counts and layouts across sources. A summary of the
dataset composition and a complete list of included sources are provided in Appendix B.
Preprocessing Pipeline
We apply a standardized preprocessing pipeline to all recordings. Power
line noise is removed via a notch filter at 50 or 60 Hz depending on the recording country. EEG
recordings are then bandpass filtered between 0.5 and 75 Hz and resampled to fs = 200 Hz. Each
channel is z-score normalized to account for cross-dataset variability in electrode impedance and
amplifier gain. For datasets that provide digitized 3D electrode locations, we use the recorded
coordinates directly; for those that specify only channel labels (e.g., the 10–20 system), we assign
coordinates from the corresponding standard montage in MNE-Python [39]. In addition, amplitude
values exceeding ± 15 standard deviations are clipped to suppress residual artifacts, as in [94]. Each
recording is segmented into non-overlapping windows of either 10 or 60 seconds depending on the
source task. Among the 60-second segments, those whose token count exceeds 4,096 are further split
into 30-second chunks for training efficiency, preserving contiguous temporal structure.
Implementation Details
We train all models with AdamW [77]. The learning rate follows a cosine
schedule with linear warmup, and the teacher encoder is updated via an EMA with a cosine momentum
schedule. We pretrain four model sizes: SPERA-Tiny (18M), SPERA-Small (48M), SPERA-Base
(194M), and SPERA-Large (396M). Full hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix C.1.

4.3
Downstream Task Results
Table 2 summarizes LPFT performance across the nine downstream tasks and four SPERA model
scales. Per-task Welch tests over five seeds, Holm-corrected across the nine comparisons, find
significant gains for SPERA-Base on five tasks: BCIC2020-3, TUEV, BCIC-IV-2a, ISRUC, and
TUAB. PhysioNet-MI is borderline (pHolm = 0.052) and no task shows a significant loss. Among the
significant differences, the largest gains are observed on TUEV (+0.078; 6-class clinical event) and
BCIC2020-3 (+0.050; 5-class imagined speech). The differences on FACED, MAT, and Mumtaz did
not reach statistical significance after Holm correction. Mumtaz in particular shows limited headroom,
with balanced accuracy remaining generally high across foundation models and model scales. SPERA
performs consistently on both BCIC-IV-2a (22 channels) and PhysioNet-MI (64 channels), two
motor-imagery tasks with different electrode layouts. Per-task differences with confidence intervals,
for both SPERA-Base and SPERA-Large, are reported in Appendix D.10.

Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off
Factorized attention reduces the matrix-multiplication cost substan-
tially at our sequence length (N= 3,840), from 653 GFLOPs for full attention to 399 GFLOPs, despite
having more parameters due to the additional axis-specific projections (50.6M vs. 37.9M). The hybrid
architecture lies between the two at 462.5 GFLOPs, reflecting its combination of factorized blocks
with periodic full-attention blocks. Here, matrix-multiplication FLOPs include linear projections and
the attention matrix multiplications, with one multiply-accumulate counted as two FLOPs.
The Legendre implementation has little effect on the matrix-multiplication cost but strongly affects
realized throughput. Under full attention, the pairwise Legendre bias requires an N × N score-bias
matrix and prevents the fused attention path used in our implementation (i.e., FlashAttention via
PyTorch SDPA), reducing throughput to 20,260 tok/s despite nearly identical matrix-multiplication
FLOP counts for the bias- and anchor-based implementations (653.0 vs. 653.1 GFLOPs). The anchor
encoding instead injects geometry features into the queries and keys while preserving the fused
attention path, reaching 75,769 tok/s. In factorized attention, the smaller attention matrices make the
9


explicit pairwise bias practical, achieving 97,043 tok/s compared with 90,714 tok/s for the anchor
encoding.
These observations motivate our final design: we use the anchor encoding in full-attention blocks
to preserve throughput and the pairwise Legendre bias in factorized layers, where its computational
cost is small. The resulting model achieves accuracy comparable to that of the bias-only hybrid
configuration while providing 1.78× higher throughput (92,866 vs. 52,263 tok/s) and 14% lower
peak memory (1.95 vs. 2.26 GB), at the cost of a 0.005 decrease in average balanced accuracy.

5
Limitations and Future Work
While our scaling analysis covers four model sizes from 18M to 396M parameters, we did not
investigate scaling at the billion-parameter scale due to limited computational resources. Furthermore,
the diminishing returns observed beyond 194M suggest that further gains may require expanded
data rather than parameters alone, but we did not systematically disentangle the contributions of
corpus size, model capacity, and architectural priors to the observed scaling behavior. A systematic
characterization of these factors remains an important direction for future investigation. Beyond scale,
the spherical prior assumes surface electrodes; extending it to intracranial recordings would require
incorporating radial information, as discussed in Appendix F.5.
We evaluate SPERA on nine diverse downstream tasks spanning clinical, cognitive, and BCI domains;
however, the tasks are focused on classification problems over fixed-duration EEG segments ranging
from 3 to 30 seconds. Further validation is therefore needed to assess generalization to continu-
ous decoding, regression targets, and long-horizon clinical prediction. In addition, demographic
metadata such as age, sex, ethnicity, and clinical status are not consistently available across our
pretraining datasets, limiting a comprehensive fairness analysis of the learned representations across
subpopulations. Addressing these gaps will be important before clinical or consumer deployment.
6
Conclusion
We present SPERA, a foundation model for EEG that learns representations through latent-space
prediction. SPERA encodes scalp electrode geometry into attention through a Legendre-polynomial
spatial prior, supported by a hybrid attention backbone and a relational spectral regularizer.
Across nine downstream tasks, SPERA-Base exceeds the strongest baseline (REVE-Base; Table 2)
by 0.035 in average balanced accuracy. The largest significant gains are observed on fine-grained
multi-class tasks, including imagined speech and clinical event classification. The scaling analysis
further shows that SPERA-Base exceeds REVE-Large under LP despite using less than half the
parameters. Controlled ablations show that latent prediction outperforms a matched reconstruction
objective and that the Legendre prior outperforms the alternative geometry encodings we evaluated.
Overall, the results support latent-space prediction, geometry-aware spatial modeling, and frequency-
aware supervision as complementary components for EEG representation learning across heteroge-
neous datasets and electrode montages. These findings provide a basis for future work on scalable
and transferable EEG representation learning.

A
Computing Resources
To ensure reproducibility, we report the detailed computing environment, as follows:
• Hardware: HP Z8 Fury G5 Workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon w5-3425 CPU, 128
GB of RAM, and dual NVIDIA RTX 5000 Ada Generation GPUs.
• Software & Environment: The environment is set up on Ubuntu 24.04.4 LTS and Python
3.10.20, supported by CUDA Toolkit 13.0 and cuDNN 9.15 for hardware acceleration.
Model implementation and training are built upon PyTorch 2.10.0 and the Hugging Face
ecosystem, utilizing transformers 5.3.0 and accelerate 1.13.0. Data preprocessing is
performed using MNE-Python 1.11.0.
• Training Efficiency & Cost: To maximize training efficiency, we employed distributed data
parallel (DDP) across both GPUs and utilized Bfloat16 (BF16) mixed-precision training.
The total computational cost for pretraining our largest model is approximately 287 GPU
hours.
• Code & Model Availability: Our codebase is provided through the repository linked in the
paper, including training, evaluation, and preprocessing scripts.

PhysioNet [38], NSRR [159], TUH [91], Brainclinics (https://brainclinics.com/resources;
see also [145]), and the Max Planck Institute [10]. Table 5 reports subject counts adjusted for known
cross-dataset overlaps, and Table 6 lists per-dataset counts. To avoid double-counting, known subject-

OpenNeuro datasets ds007169 [15]/ds007262 [14]). In addition, to prevent any evaluation leakage,
subjects reserved for downstream tasks (TUAB and TUEV) are excluded from the TUH corpus. For
HSP [140], we use the small training subset released for the PhysioNet Challenge 2026 [114]. Unless
otherwise noted, reported hours reflect the pretraining corpus after these exclusions.

the cumulative target-token count reaches the per-update budget of 131,072 tokens (Table 7), so the

Figure 3 shows the pretraining loss curve of the SPERA-Base (194M) model. Each epoch realizes
∼9.45B input tokens (∼6.25B context, ∼3.20B target), or ∼98.4% of the ∼9.60B nominal token
count derived from corpus statistics (Table 5). The remaining ∼1.6% corresponds to rare (C, Pt)
shape buckets that do not accumulate a full microbatch within the dataloader’s pending window and
are dropped by the shape-aware batcher. The residual is small because the unit that is dropped is a
rare shape bucket rather than a dataset: even our smallest dataset (∼0.91 h, 8 channels) produces
∼26K tokens, exceeding the per-microbatch budget. The resulting loss curve (Figure 3) converges
smoothly and stabilizes around 0.05 by the end of training.
D
Detailed Downstream Task Results
In this section, we describe each downstream dataset, its preprocessing procedure, and the full
experimental results. For all datasets, we follow the preprocessing pipelines of prior works [155, 57,
150, 94] to ensure direct comparison with baseline models. While we report balanced accuracy as the
primary metric in the main text, we additionally report task-specific metrics commonly used in prior
EEG foundation model evaluations [155, 57, 150, 94]. For multi-class classification tasks, we report
Cohen’s κ and weighted F1-score in addition to balanced accuracy. For binary classification tasks,
we report AUC-PR and AUROC in addition to balanced accuracy. SPERA results are reported as
mean ± standard deviation over five random seeds; baseline statistics follow the evaluation protocols
of the source publications. Each downstream task table includes only baseline models whose original
studies report results on that task. A dash indicates that the corresponding model does not report that
dataset or metric. Unless otherwise specified, all results are obtained using the SPERA-Base (194M)
model.

Hz. Each trial consists of a 3-second segment, yielding 6,000 samples in total with predefined
train/validation/test splits of 60/10/10 trials per class.
As shown in Table 9, SPERA achieves 0.6504 balanced accuracy, with Cohen’s κ of 0.5630 and

D.2
Motor Imagery Classification
BCIC-IV-2a [143] is a 4-class motor imagery dataset comprising nine subjects performing left hand,
right hand, both feet, and tongue imagery. EEG signals are recorded across two sessions using 22
electrodes at 250 Hz. Each trial is cropped to the 2–6 s segment, bandpass filtered to 0.5–99.5 Hz,
and resampled to 200 Hz, yielding 5,184 4-second samples. Following prior work [94], Euclidean
alignment is additionally applied. We adopt a subject-independent split, with subjects 1–5, 6–7, and
8–9 used for training, validation, and test, respectively.
PhysioNet-MI [38, 122] is a 4-class motor imagery dataset recorded from 64 channels at 160 Hz,
covering left fist, right fist, both fists, and both feet imagery. We extract 4-second segments yielding
9,837 samples, and apply a subject-independent split with subjects 1–70, 71–89, and 90–109 for
training, validation, and test, respectively. All recordings are loaded at 128 Hz with a 64 Hz low-pass
filter and subsequently resampled to 200 Hz.
The detailed experimental results for both motor imagery tasks are summarized in Table 10. SPERA
improves over REVE-Base on both BCIC-IV-2a (+0.0420) and PhysioNet-MI (+0.0246) in balanced
accuracy, with similar trends in the other two metrics. The improvement is consistent across the two
datasets despite their differing channel counts (22 vs. 64) and acquisition protocols.

Mental Stress Detection
Mental arithmetic (MAT) [164] is a 2-class mental stress dataset comprising 36 subjects recorded
using 20 channels at 500 Hz, distinguishing resting state from active mental arithmetic. EEG signals
are bandpass filtered (0.5–45 Hz), resampled to 200 Hz, and segmented into 1,707 5-second samples.
Subjects 1–28, 29–32, and 33–36 are used for training, validation, and test, respectively.
The experimental results are shown in Table 12. SPERA exceeds REVE-Base by +0.0118 in balanced
accuracy and +0.0454 in AUROC, while its AUC-PR is lower.
D.5
Sleep Staging
ISRUC [63] is a 5-class sleep staging dataset comprising 100 subjects recorded using 6 channels
at 200 Hz. EEG signals are segmented into 30-second epochs and annotated according to the
AASM standard (Wake, N1, N2, N3, REM). Subjects 1–80, 81–90, and 91–100 are used for training,
validation, and test, respectively. Subject 8 was excluded from the final dataset due to missing requisite
EEG channels (e.g., F3, F4), resulting in a total of 88,140 samples. Sleep staging is commonly
framed as a sequence-to-sequence classification problem with a sequence length of 20 consecutive
30-second epochs to model sleep stage transitions [107]. Following the protocol of CBraMod [150],
we adopt this standard setting and additionally report single-segment classification results to assess
the quality of our pretrained representations without sequential context. We further include two
widely recognized sleep-staging-specific baselines, DeepSleepNet [142] and U-Sleep [103], in order
to compare against classical methods established in the sleep staging literature.
Full classification results are summarized in Table 13. The single-segment variant alone (0.8093)
exceeds the strongest baseline (CSBrain, 0.7925) in balanced accuracy, indicating that the gain
does not require the sequence-to-sequence formulation. The sequence-to-sequence variant further
improves performance to 0.8137. These results indicate that SPERA remains effective on ISRUC,

Additional Ablation Experiments
E.1
Pretraining Objective and Corpus
The ablations in Table 3 vary architectural components while holding the primary prediction objective
and corpus fixed. Here we vary those two factors instead, to separate the contribution of latent-space
prediction from that of the pretraining data. Both variants use the SPERA-Small backbone and follow
the ablation protocol of Section 4.5: LP on PhysioNet-MI, ISRUC, and TUAB. Each configuration is
evaluated over five random seeds.
For the reconstruction variant, we replace the latent prediction objective with input-space recon-
struction. The predictor outputs are decoded back to raw patches by a linear head and the model
is trained against the masked input patches, so that the target is computed from the raw signal. All
other components, including the Legendre prior, the hybrid backbone, and the relational spectral
regularizer, are unchanged.
For the REVE corpus variant, we restrict pretraining to dataset sources shared with REVE [94] to
assess the effect of this corpus restriction on SPERA’s downstream performance. The proxy comprises
81 datasets from our corpus that are also listed in REVE’s pretraining corpus, totaling 61,191 h from
22,994 subjects, comparable in duration to the approximately 60,000 h reported for REVE. The 25
excluded datasets account for 18,473 h and contribute 6,054 additional subjects; 16,367 h are sleep
recordings from MESA and the HSP subset, and the remaining 2,106 h come from 23 datasets.
Replacing latent prediction with input-space reconstruction lowers average balanced accuracy by
0.022 with the backbone, corpus, and token budget held fixed, and the drop is consistent across
all three tasks. Pretraining on the REVE corpus proxy lowers the average by 0.005. Specifically,
PhysioNet-MI and TUAB are nearly identical, while ISRUC is lower by 0.016. This is consistent
with the composition of the excluded data, 89% of which is sleep recordings, and ISRUC is the only
sleep-staging task among the three.

E.3

union of the masked regions, assuming independence between temporal and spatial masking. We
define five difficulty levels (ρ ≈0.27, 0.34, 0.45, 0.65, 0.75); detailed hyperparameter ranges for each

Analysis of the Legendre Spherical Prior
F.1
Geometry Encoding Ablations
Building on the electrode geometry ablation in Section 4.5, we evaluate a broader set of alternative
geometry encodings. Everything outside the geometry encoding is held fixed. Coordinate Embedding
Only removes the pairwise prior and retains the linear embedding of 3D electrode coordinates.
Anisotropic SH replaces the rotation-invariant Legendre expansion with a general spherical-harmonic
(SH) prior that permits direction-dependent interactions and contains the isotropic Legendre family
as a special case. Gaussian (Geodesic) replaces the expansion with a radial basis function (RBF)
kernel of geodesic electrode distance, with learnable width and amplitude. REVE 4D Encoding
ports the coupled spatio-temporal encoding of REVE [94] as published, which also replaces our
temporal RoPE. Since that conflates spatial and temporal handling, we derive REVE Spatial Encoding,
which imports only the 3D coordinate component and leaves RoPE in place, isolating the geometry
comparison.
The results are summarized in Table 24. The Legendre prior is the strongest configuration on all three
tasks, and REVE’s encoding provides no transfer advantage in our backbone. Replacing REVE’s
joint 4D encoding with its spatial component while retaining RoPE improves performance on all
three tasks, with the largest increase on ISRUC (0.593 to 0.691). At matched temporal handling,
REVE’s Fourier encoding is comparable to our linear coordinate embedding, and the improvement
comes from adding the prior on top. The geodesic Gaussian fixes the kernel to a monotone decay
in distance, whereas our expansion learns its profile end-to-end; the learned kernel is not monotone
(Appendix F.2). The anisotropic SH prior also underperforms the isotropic Legendre prior (0.658 vs.
0.677 average), providing no empirical benefit from the added directional flexibility under the present
setting.

Pairwise-Bias and Anchor-Based Implementations
The two implementations differ in how geometry enters attention and therefore in computational cost.
In the pairwise Legendre bias form, the Legendre values Pℓ(e⊤
i ej) are computed once per forward

Extension to Intracranial Recordings
For scalp EEG, electrodes are distributed over the scalp surface, and we therefore represent their
locations using unit-normalized directions. For iEEG and sEEG, where electrodes occupy the brain
volume, radial position also becomes informative. The construction can be extended to the solid ball
B3 while retaining the same rotation-invariant angular structure. On the solid ball, separable solutions
of the Laplacian eigenproblem consist of a radial part given by spherical Bessel functions jℓand an
angular spherical-harmonic part. Using the corresponding rotation-invariant angular form yields

the radial wavenumber, and wℓn are learnable coefficients. The angular Legendre structure is retained
and radius enters through a learnable radial basis; the surface prior used in this paper is the special
case of a shared radius.
Two practical obstacles remain: intracranial electrode positions require coregistration to a common
anatomical space, such as the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template, before a shared radial
basis is meaningful, and the high-gamma content that carries much of the information in intracranial
recordings lies outside our 0.5–75 Hz preprocessing pipeline, so the tokenizer would need revision.
We leave this extension to future work.

Physiological Ocular Artifacts
We further evaluate robustness to ocular artifacts on PhysioNet-MI and TUAB using electrooculo-
graphic (EOG) waveforms from EEGdenoiseNet [160]; ISRUC is excluded because the ocular artifact
injection protocol is not appropriate for sleep EEG. Ocular artifacts are projected onto each montage

vertical and horizontal EOG waveforms are normalized, projected using these spatial fields, and
combined using the reported horizontal-to-vertical saccade amplitude ratio. A shared intermittent
envelope leaves at least 30% of each trial without added artifacts, after which the combined artifact is
scaled to the target SNR based on trial-level mean EEG and artifact power across channels and time.
